The Friday-night quote request: why a three-day reply loses the customer

A customer writes on Friday night asking for a quote. The answer arrives on Tuesday. By then they have talked to two competitors and made their choice. Nobody in the company did anything wrong: the request just sat there. It is the most common loss, and the hardest to see, because it leaves no trace.

In short

Someone asking for a quote usually writes to several companies and picks the first that answers seriously. In a study published by Harvard Business Review, companies that contacted the customer within an hour were nearly seven times more likely to reach a decision maker than those that waited one hour longer. An automated sales process closes the gap with three parts: instant reply, quote follow-up and reactivation of old customers, with a person approving messages before they go out.

Customer response time: the real weak spot

Someone asking for a quote usually writes to several companies and picks the first that comes back with a serious answer. Price matters, but it comes second. A request that waits three days has usually already lost to the company that answered within an hour, even if that one was more expensive.

This is not just a feeling. In 2011 Harvard Business Review published an analysis of how US companies respond to online enquiries: only 37% answered within an hour, 23% never answered at all and, among those that did, the average wait was 42 hours. Companies that contacted the customer within an hour were nearly seven times more likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker than those that waited just one hour longer (source: Harvard Business Review, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads"). Every market is different, but anyone running a small business will recognise the pattern.

The problem is not unwillingness to reply. It is that requests arrive when nobody is looking: in the evening, over the weekend, while the owner is at a client's and the sales team is on the phone.

Three losses that appear in no accounts

  • Salespeople buried in time-wasting calls. People who only want information, who have no budget, who will never buy take up the same hours as those ready to sign. Good leads wait behind useless ones.
  • Quotes sent and never followed up. Silence after a quote is rarely a no: more often it is a busy person. Without a reminder the offer sits in an inbox until it expires.
  • An archive of old contacts gathering dust. Customers from two years ago, requests never closed, offers left hanging. Selling to someone who already knows you costs far less than finding a new customer, but nobody has time to go back through them.

What an automated sales process is (and why it is not spam)

An automated sales process is a set of workflows that takes every incoming request, acknowledges it at once, sorts it and follows it through to the close, without relying on anyone's memory. When AI in sales comes up, many people picture mass messaging. It is the opposite: nothing is sent in bulk, and every request finds someone, or something, that deals with it at once. It has three parts:

  1. Instant reply and lead qualification, at any hour. As soon as a request comes in, a personalised acknowledgement goes out. A few targeted questions (what is needed, by when, with what budget) separate those ready to buy from those just looking. You immediately get the leads that deserve a phone call.
  2. Reactivating old customers. The system goes back through the archive and prepares a targeted message for people you have not heard from in a while: an open offer, a product that complements the one already bought, an approaching deadline.
  3. Automatic quote follow-up. Every quote has a follow-up date. If there is no answer after a few days, you get a reminder with the draft already written. Nothing gets forgotten again.
The rule I recommend: AI prepares, a person approves. Faster than writing everything from scratch, without losing control over what goes out in the company's name.

A real example: from request to phone call

On my own website I use a workflow built with n8n, an open-source tool running on a Raspberry Pi, every day: when someone subscribes to the newsletter, it checks that the email is valid, adds it to the right list, shows the confirmation straight away and alerts me on Telegram. Nobody has to remember anything. Applied to a quote request, the same pattern looks like this:

  1. The request arrives through the website form or by email, even at eleven on a Friday night.
  2. Within a minute an acknowledgement goes out with the customer's name, when they will receive the quote and two questions to prepare it better.
  3. AI reads the request and the answers, sorts it (ready to buy, just information, not a fit) and sends a summary with a draft reply to your phone.
  4. You approve or edit it. The quote goes into a single list with a follow-up date, and the reminder arrives on its own.

On Monday morning there is no inbox to clear: there is a list of customers already contacted, sorted by priority.

What you do not need

You do not need to change your management software or buy a tool costing hundreds a month. Most of the time the pieces already exist (the website form, email, a spreadsheet) and just need connecting. Before building anything, though, you have to know where you lose the most.

Where to start: three questions

  • On average, how long passes between a request and the first reply? And for the ones that arrive on Friday night?
  • How many quotes from the last quarter were followed up at least once?
  • When did you last write to customers from two years ago, and why?

If you cannot answer with a number, you have found the first gap. The next step is a sales process analysis: we trace a request from first contact to signature and work out how much revenue is left on the table.

Frequently asked questions

How should you reply to a customer who asks for a quote out of hours? With a short acknowledgement straight away: the request has arrived, when they will get the quote, and one or two questions to prepare it better. The customer knows someone is on it and stops looking elsewhere.

How many days should you wait before following up an unanswered quote? A practical rule is a first follow-up after two or three working days and a second after a week, each time with a concrete reason (a clarification, availability, a deadline). Beyond that, it is better to ask openly whether the project is still active.

Does AI reply to customers instead of me? Only if you want it to. An automatic acknowledgement is enough for the first reply; for the rest, I recommend that AI prepares the draft and a person approves it before it is sent.

Do I need a CRM to follow up quotes? Not necessarily. For many small companies a single list updated automatically is enough. A CRM makes sense once volume grows.

How long does it take to get started? It depends on the tools already in use. The first piece, usually the instant reply or the quote follow-up, can be built in a few days.
